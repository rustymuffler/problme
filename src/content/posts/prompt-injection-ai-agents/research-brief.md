# Research Brief: Prompt injection: the attack that's already hiding in the pages, DNS records, and inboxes AI agents read
Date: 2026-07-16

## Key Statistics and Data

- Google observed a **32% relative increase** in malicious prompt-injection-category detections across Common Crawl (2-3 billion mostly static pages) between November 2025 and February 2026. Attacks are characterized as "limited sophistication" and mostly individual experiments, not coordinated campaigns, but trending up. — Source: https://blog.google/security/prompt-injections-web/ (Published: 2026, within last 12 months)
- Anthropic's Best-of-N adaptive attacker testing found Claude Opus 4.5's browser extension has an attack success rate of roughly **1%** against prompt injection, down from **10.8%** for Claude Sonnet 4.5 under earlier safeguards (an earlier figure of 1.4% appears in some secondary coverage of an interim safeguard state; the ~1% figure is Anthropic's own reported current number). Anthropic explicitly states even ~1% "still represents meaningful risk." — Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/prompt-injection-defenses (Published: 2025-11-24)
- In a large indirect-prompt-injection dataset study of real-world web pages, researchers catalogued 22 distinct payload-engineering techniques across 12 real-world case studies. Visible plaintext was the most common delivery method (37.8%), followed by HTML attribute cloaking (19.8%) and CSS rendering suppression (16.9%). Social engineering framing dominated jailbreak-style payloads (85.2%), and 75.8% of pages carried only a single injected prompt. — Source: https://unit42.paloaltonetworks.com/ai-agent-prompt-injection/ (Published: 2026-03-03)
- A steganographic image-injection study reported an overall attack success rate of 24.3% across GPT-4V, Claude, and LLaVA when malicious instructions were hidden inside images via steganography, rising to 31.8% for neural steganography methods specifically. This is an **academic red-team study**, not a documented in-the-wild incident count. — Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22304 (flag: exact publication date not fully confirmed in search results; arXiv ID format suggests 2025; treat as within the 2-year window but verify before citing a specific date)

## Counterarguments to Address

- **"Model providers are already solving this with better training and classifiers, so defense-in-depth at the application/user level is redundant."** — Anthropic's own data undercuts this: even with RL training and real-time classifiers, Claude Opus 4.5 still has a ~1% attack success rate against an adaptive attacker, and Anthropic itself states this "still represents meaningful risk" (https://www.anthropic.com/research/prompt-injection-defenses). Acknowledge the real progress, then note that 1% of a high enough volume of agent actions, or a single successful attack against a consequential action, is still a real failure mode. Model-level improvement and system-level defense-in-depth are complementary, not substitutes.
- **"DNS TXT record injection is a theoretical, over-hyped vector that isn't worth covering in depth."** — This was Richard's own provisional take going in, and the research does not fully support it as stated. The 0din.ai writeup documents a concrete, working proof-of-concept against Claude Code specifically (published 2026-06-25), where a malicious payload was fetched at runtime from a DNS TXT record and was invisible to code review and static scanners. It is a demonstrated, dated technique against a real agentic coding tool, not purely hypothetical, even though it is not (yet) documented as a mass-exploited-in-the-wild campaign the way the hidden-webpage vector is. The article should correct the "possibly overhyped" framing to something like "real and demonstrated, but currently narrower and less widespread than the webpage and email vectors."
- **"This is fearmongering; most of these attacks are harmless pranks, not real damage."** — Google's own Common Crawl research found "harmless pranks" is in fact the single most prevalent category found on the live web today (https://blog.google/security/prompt-injections-web/). Address this directly: acknowledge most in-the-wild attempts today are unsophisticated, while noting that EchoLeak (CVE-2025-32711, a critical zero-click Microsoft 365 Copilot vulnerability disclosed June 2025) and the January 2026 disclosures against IBM Bob, Superhuman, Notion AI, and Claude Cowork show the ceiling on severity is not hypothetical, even if EchoLeak itself was patched before confirmed in-the-wild exploitation.

## Verification Tasks From Interview Notes

**1. Technique prevalence ranking (interview Section 4) — corrected ranking below.**

Richard's provisional ranking is directionally right on three points and needs one explicit correction on DNS:

- **Most prevalent / best documented today: hidden content in webpages, confirmed.** Google's own Common Crawl measurement (2-3 billion pages, Nov 2025-Feb 2026, 32% relative increase in malicious-category detections) and Unit42's 12-case-study/22-technique catalogue both confirm this is the largest, best-measured vector by volume. This matches Richard's lawsofux.com case directly. **Confirmed, keep as-is.**
- **Email/calendar-invite payloads aimed at AI email assistants: confirmed as high-severity, though lower-volume than the webpage vector.** EchoLeak (CVE-2025-32711, disclosed June 2025, CVSS 9.3) is the strongest single documented case: a zero-click indirect prompt injection via a crafted email against Microsoft 365 Copilot, patched before confirmed in-the-wild exploitation. Varonis's "Reprompt" disclosure and SafeBreach's Gemini research (messaging-app notifications, hidden Gmail instructions) add further documented, named cases. **Confirmed as high-severity; note it's fewer documented incidents than the webpage vector but higher average severity per incident.**
- **DNS TXT record injection: correct the "possibly overhyped" framing, do not drop it, do not overstate it either.** This is the most important correction from the research. 0din.ai (Mozilla's Zero Day Investigative Network) published a dated, concrete, technically detailed proof-of-concept (2026-06-25) against Claude Code specifically: a GitHub repo with a deliberately-failing setup script that, on Claude's autonomous error-recovery attempt, ran a `dig +short TXT` lookup and executed a base64-decoded reverse-shell payload that never existed in the repo itself, making it invisible to code review and static scanning. This is real and demonstrated, not purely theoretical. However, it is a researcher-disclosed proof-of-concept against one agentic coding tool, not (based on available search results) a vector with multiple independent in-the-wild incident reports the way the webpage and email vectors have. **Revised framing for the article: "real and demonstrated against at least one major agentic coding tool, but narrower in documented scope than the webpage and email vectors, worth including precisely because it's the vector most likely to bypass code review and static scanning."**
- **Images/PDFs: confirmed as real and growing, correctly framed by Richard.** Academic red-team research (arXiv 2507.22304) demonstrates a 24.3% average attack success rate (up to 31.8% for neural steganography) hiding instructions in images across GPT-4V, Claude, and LLaVA. This is lab research demonstrating feasibility at meaningful success rates, not yet matched by a named in-the-wild incident in the search results reviewed, so frame it as "demonstrated at meaningful success rates in security research, expected to grow as multimodal agents do more OCR and document parsing" rather than claiming a specific in-the-wild incident. **[UNVERIFIED — needs source]** for any specific named in-the-wild image/PDF injection incident; none surfaced in this research pass.
- **SEO-poisoned pages: confirmed as overlapping with the hidden-webpage vector, correctly framed by Richard.** Threat-modeling.com and Cybernews both document attackers optimizing pages for both search ranking and AI-summarizer ingestion simultaneously; Google's Common Crawl research includes an explicit "SEO manipulation" category. **Confirmed, keep as a related but distinct sub-case of the webpage vector, as Richard's draft framed it.**
- **Untrusted survey/form input: confirmed as the least attacker-in-the-wild-documented of the set.** No named in-the-wild incident surfaced in this research pass specifically for form/survey input as an external attack vector (as distinct from a developer building a vulnerable RAG/chat integration, which is a design-mistake category, not an attacker campaign). **Confirmed as Richard framed it: a developer-mistake category more than an active attacker vector.**

**2. Defense framing (interview Section 5) — sourced and confirmed.**

Richard's defense-in-depth argument (treat external content as untrusted data, structural separation of instructions vs. content, minimum tool permissions, human-in-the-loop for consequential actions, logging/monitoring) is well-supported by current, citable sources, not just an agent-drafted opinion:

- OWASP LLM01:2025's own mitigation list independently arrives at the same five ideas: constrain model behavior, segregate external content, enforce least-privilege access controls, require human approval for consequential actions, and log/monitor. https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm01-prompt-injection/
- Simon Willison's lethal trifecta post makes the structural argument for *why* instruction-only defenses fail (models cannot reliably distinguish instruction sources; defenses must prevent the trifecta from forming or block consequential actions structurally, not rely on the model refusing). https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jun/16/the-lethal-trifecta/
- Anthropic's own published defenses combine exactly this pattern in practice: RL training against injected content, real-time classifiers scanning untrusted content entering the context window, and human-confirmation gating before consequential actions, while explicitly stating a non-zero attack success rate remains and "still represents meaningful risk." https://www.anthropic.com/research/prompt-injection-defenses
- Meta's "Agents Rule of Two" is a second, independently-developed major-lab framework reaching the same conclusion via a different lens: an agent should satisfy no more than two of three risk properties (process untrusted input, access sensitive data, take consequential action) per session. https://ai.meta.com/blog/practical-ai-agent-security/

**Conclusion: Richard's Section 5 framing is well-grounded and does not need softening.** It can be presented as informed opinion backed by four independent, citable sources (OWASP, Willison, Anthropic, Meta) that converge on the same structural argument.
